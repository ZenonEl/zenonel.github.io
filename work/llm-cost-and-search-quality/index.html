W-05Case

The cost of an answer: cache, a narrow check and a search that failed its measurement

The bot answers franchise staff in their work chats from a knowledge base and stays silent or calls a person on everything else. When the base grew nearly fourfold, from 85 to 337 entries, an answer took 22 seconds and cost $0.13. I found where the money went: the provider's cache worked every other time, and the verifier read the whole base for the sake of a few entries. On 8 August, on live traffic, an answer with a cache hit cost $0.0310 against $0.0968 without one, and segmented search, which management asked to check as a saving, was rejected by measurement.

The bot and who built it

The bot lives in the work groups of a flower shop franchise. It has three agents: a gatekeeper decides whether the question is its business, a responder answers from the knowledge base, and a verifier checks the answer against the base. The whole base sits right in the prompt; there is no separate search. Outside its competence the bot stays silent. On sensitive topics, whether money, royalties, revenue, prices, personal data or other categories the client sets up in the admin panel, it answers only with an approved template, and if there is none, it calls an operator.

The base in the prompt was a deliberate choice. At the start, one franchisee's chat history gave 231 question-and-answer pairs, about 64 of them genuinely recurring, and at that size separate search is unnecessary: the model sees the whole base and finds an answer by meaning rather than by matching words. Search over the base was planned for later, after 300 entries.

A colleague built most of the first version, the three-agent pipeline and the base in the prompt. On 17 June I audited that MVP, extended it and ran a live test in a group, and after that I mostly refined the bot. On 13 July the bot went to production and on 15 July live mode was switched on. Management set tasks and made decisions, the colleague wrote 50 of the 139 commits, and a representative on the client side tested it. AI agents wrote the code, every change went through subagent review until LGTM, and production releases went out only on an explicit go, in a quiet window, with a database backup.

From 7 July every model call recorded the model, tokens, latency and the real cost from the provider's response. Without that accounting there would have been nothing to measure later.

On 29 July the bill hit the limit

That day the production key ran out of limit. The investigation found several causes at once. Every answer reserved 65,536 tokens because no answer limit was set. The base had grown from 85 to 337 entries, and about 43 thousand tokens went into every request. An answer cost $0.13 and took 22 seconds, and the provider's prompt cache hardly ever hit.

The cache lived on one route

A measurement cost about 30 roubles and showed that the provider's cache turns on by itself but lives on a specific route, while requests are balanced across routes, so hits came every other time. I pinned the route but kept a fallback: if the main one is down, the bot answers at a higher price, but it answers.

The change went to production the same day. The first 9 requests cost 79 roubles instead of the 207 they would have cost without the cache: minus 62%. That is a computed comparison on the same requests, not a separate before run, and the questions came in a dense burst, so for a month I forecast about 40%. The cache hardly affects speed.

Two things surfaced later. On 30 July management changed the model lineup: an answer cost 14.5 roubles instead of 31.9 and took 11 seconds instead of 21.5, but the new verifier's automatic cache did not work at all, zero hits across 495 thousand tokens. And any edit to the base reset the cache for everyone: a new entry in the middle of the prompt raised a call from $0.0153 to $0.0705, so the colleague moved fresh entries to the end.

On 8 August, on live traffic, 85% of input came from the cache, and an answer with a cache hit cost $0.0310 against $0.0968 without one and arrived in 9.4 seconds instead of 15.3.

An answer scheme of our own, checked in steps

Making an answer cheaper without losing accuracy did not work at the first attempt, and by 31 July I had put together a scheme in steps. First I labelled the live logs: 17 verifier rejections and 55 refusals. The most frequent class of real errors turned out to be invented promises. In parallel, nine research reports went through the options, from vector search to custom detectors, and I dropped everything that does not pay off at our volume: own hardware, a vector database, cascades of three or more steps, fine-tuning on refusals.

In this scheme the responder returns claims with entry numbers and verbatim quotes, and code checks that the entry exists and contains the quote. The verifier reads not the whole base but the cited entries plus a few entries found by the draft's text, which also catches entries the answer contradicts. If the narrow check rejects an answer, it is checked against the full base, and the decision to answer fully, partly or hand over to a person is made by code against a threshold; money, deadlines and accounting stay on a strict policy.

I wrote the answer contract myself on 5 August, and it went through seven review rounds. The next day a $1.34 output-length measurement showed that at a 4096-token limit an answer in the new format gets truncated, so the limit went up to 8192 and the contract was switched on at 12:51. The three-outcome gate went to production on 6 August in shadow mode: it computes and logs its decision but does not affect sending. It was never made live: in the second half of August two questions came in over eight days, so there were no statistics to switch on.

The verifier read the whole base for a few entries

The narrow check gave the most visible saving. Two thirds of the bill came from the verifier: like the responder, it was given the whole base. I proposed giving it only the entries the answer cited, plus a lexical backstop in case a citation was missed: about fifteen hundred tokens instead of 42 thousand. The colleague implemented it and I reviewed it. If the narrow check rejects an answer, it is rechecked against the full base, so the saving cannot silently reject a correct answer.

On 6 August I switched the flag on. The verifier went from $0.0600 to $0.0045 per call, input fell from 38,268 to 2,970 tokens, and latency from 7.6 to 2.7 seconds. The sample is small: 37 calls before and 5 after.

Segmented search failed its measurement

Management asked me to check segmented search: split the base by topic and give the model only the relevant part. I put together a set of 37 questions, 25 already answered and 12 from the queue. A model labelled the reference against the full base and I spot-checked it by hand; the labelling itself cost 42 roubles.

The segmented scheme came out worst: the right entry in the top five in 59% of cases against 76, 82 and 82% for lexical, vector and hybrid search and 100% for the full base, answer coverage at 40% against 49, 62 and 57%, and 4.4 seconds per request. It guessed the topic in 71% of cases, a wrong topic cannot be recovered, and half of the answers draw on entries from different topics.

It was the only question on which all nine reports agreed. On 31 July I recommended not adopting search and wrote down when to come back to it: coverage of at least 95% in the top twenty. The specification planned a switch to search after 300 entries, and the base had already passed that, but with the full base in the prompt and a working cache there is no point changing the scheme before the base grows three to five times. So RAG was deliberately left out and our own mechanics stayed: the full base in the prompt, the cache and the checks in steps.

Where the bot stands

The bot works in 20 franchise chats. From 14 July to 18 September it received 163 questions: it answered 59 itself, passed 80 to a person, stayed silent 21 times because the question was outside its area, and made three errors. Ratings are 21 positive and 6 negative. The base holds 359 entries, and all of production to date cost $9.03 across 397 model calls.

The bot saw little use: 163 questions over a little more than two months across 20 chats. Management never told me why. The base hardly grew either: the client's materials had been awaiting approval since 14 July. There was almost no live feedback from the client, unlike projects where the client tested the product and sent feedback themselves, even if through management. That is why some figures here are small: minus 62% was computed on nine requests as “would have been without the cache”, and the verifier difference was taken on five calls after the switch.

How it is built

Architecture
  • Three agents in a chain: gatekeeper, responder, verifier; the whole knowledge base in the prompt.
  • The model is called through a port, and provider specifics such as the pinned route are locked in the adapter; the balance also comes through its own port.
  • The domain does not depend on the Telegram library.
Infrastructure
  • Python, aiogram, SQLite and systemd on one server, models through OpenRouter.
  • Per-call accounting in the bot's database: model, tokens, cached input, latency and cost from the provider's response.
  • Releases in a quiet window with a database backup; downtime on restart about 5 seconds.
Fault tolerance
  • A pinned route with a fallback allowed: on failure the bot answers at a higher price, but answers.
  • When the narrow check rejects an answer, it is rechecked against the full base.
  • Outside its competence the bot stays silent or calls a person; on sensitive categories from the admin panel it answers only with an approved template.
  • Fresh base entries go to the end of the prompt so an edit does not reset the whole cache.
Data and AI
  • Gemini Flash and GPT family models through OpenRouter; all production to date $9.03.
  • Personal data and company details were cleaned out of the knowledge base; prices live in approved templates.
Decisions and assumptions
  • The full base in the prompt instead of search at the start, search only after 300 entries; once past the threshold, still no RAG until measurement shows coverage of at least 95%.
  • Cache first, search later: they compete, and search breaks the shared prompt prefix.
  • Pin the route but keep a fallback.
  • Switch on the narrow-check flag and the new answer contract on different days so the effects do not mix.
  • Reject the morphology change: parsing gaps fell from 620 to 533, but on 76 live questions the result did not change, 34 and 34.
  • The conversion rate is fixed at 100 roubles to the dollar; the specification's 300-entry threshold is not applied until the base grows three to five times.
