Commercial work

Some of the work behind my CV.

I work on backends, product integrations and applied AI features. Requirements usually reach me through management; I turn them into implementation and check the result.

These cases cover payment paths through to their real effects, an import that needed a rollback, and metrics that were measuring the wrong thing. Each page follows the work far enough to explain the decision.

Client names and private project material are omitted.

  • W-01Case

    Real orders before the official launch

    I led the build of a PrestaShop store on a stack that was new to me: AI agents wrote the code, and every change went through subagent review until LGTM before production. Work began on 24 July 2026 and the first real payment went through on 9 September. From 9 to 29 September, before the official launch and with the site hidden from search engines, the store took 101 orders, 70 of them paid, and 347,980 RUB passed through its online till.

  • W-02Case

    A finance bot that says only what it knows about the money

    I led the finance side of a Telegram back-office bot: balances, statements, scheduled payments and money notifications that the client sets up alone through a wizard. AI in the bot reads free text and writes a weekly summary; only code does the arithmetic. From 28 August to 29 September the bot sent 1,344 notifications, 1,005 of them on incoming payments, from six rules the client set up alone. What mattered most was never passing off a bank failure as an absence of money. Large sums pass through the client's accounts and a write to their bank cannot be undone, so company details and payments go through checks and gates: a wrong tax number or account has to stop before the bank, not after the money has gone to the wrong place.

  • W-03Case

    The contract path from order to fiscal receipt

    I ran the backend of a contract project: a site where a client builds an order in a configurator, pays online and receives documents in a personal cabinet. In five weeks I took it from someone else's skeleton to a production server, then through three waves of client changes. I counted a payment as finished not on the gateway's word, but only when the internal state agreed and a fiscal receipt existed.

  • W-04Case

    The product metric counted a refusal as a successful answer

    This was my first project after coming back to the team, and the first where I learned to work with AI agents. In a SaaS that shows how AI assistants see a website, the main metric looked for the brand name in the model's answer and counted a refusal as a mention: 14 of 15 where there was not a single real one. I moved it to a verification service with a model judge and two rubrics, calibrated it on a 44-case set, and carried the same principle, no grounds means refusal, into article generation.

    Production test stand; the project was dropped before a public launch

  • W-05Case

    The cost of an answer: cache, a narrow check and a search that failed its measurement

    The bot answers franchise staff in their work chats from a knowledge base and stays silent or calls a person on everything else. When the base grew nearly fourfold, from 85 to 337 entries, an answer took 22 seconds and cost $0.13. I found where the money went: the provider's cache worked every other time, and the verifier read the whole base for the sake of a few entries. On 8 August, on live traffic, an answer with a cache hit cost $0.0310 against $0.0968 without one, and segmented search, which management asked to check as a saving, was rejected by measurement.
