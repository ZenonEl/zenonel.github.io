W-02Case

A finance bot that says only what it knows about the money

I led the finance side of a Telegram back-office bot: balances, statements, scheduled payments and money notifications that the client sets up alone through a wizard. AI in the bot reads free text and writes a weekly summary; only code does the arithmetic. From 28 August to 29 September the bot sent 1,344 notifications, 1,005 of them on incoming payments, from six rules the client set up alone. What mattered most was never passing off a bank failure as an absence of money. Large sums pass through the client's accounts and a write to their bank cannot be undone, so company details and payments go through checks and gates: a wrong tax number or account has to stop before the bank, not after the money has gone to the wrong place.

The bot grew from documents into live money

The project started in summer 2026 as a document assistant: an invoice, a contract and an acceptance certificate are built by step-by-step wizards in Telegram, and the bot finds the company's own documents from a phrase such as “send me the second company's charter”. On 13 August the second stage began, the bank: balances, a categorised statement, scheduled payments, notifications and access control. On 18 August the client first logged into the production bot and has used it every day since.

Management talked to the client, and every question to the client cost time. So I closed many forks myself: I chose a safe assumption, wrote it down and moved on, and took upstairs only what could not move without the client's decision. Decisions and failure boundaries were mine, as were hands-on checks of critical paths and the call on whether a change was ready for production. AI agents wrote the code, and every change went through subagent review until LGTM.

The first job of the banking stage was someone else's code. On 14 August a colleague merged the bank webhook receiver and the access list into the main branch, 509 and 148 lines with no review, and they went straight to production. My review on 17 August found four defects. One chat that had blocked the bot broke notification delivery for everyone else, permanently. A non-ASCII character in the signature header crashed the receiver with a 500 before the signature was even checked. A second reminder about the same payment was silently swallowed. A typo in the access list removed the protection and opened the bot to everyone. My fixes went through five review rounds: the second, third and fourth found errors in the fixes themselves, and the fifth gave LGTM.

The API on paper and the API in production

The bot reads the banks through a third-party gateway. The gateway had a specification, but in production it sometimes behaved differently from the paper: an empty list could mean no transactions, one unavailable account, or a period the gateway had silently cut short. Writes are stricter still: the service has no delete, so a payment created in the client's bank contour cannot be removed through the same API.

So I investigated every mismatch first and only then decided how to live with it. A wide statement request returned 25 transactions, while month-by-month requests summed to 275. Thirty-four manual measurements revealed a hidden rule: the gateway cuts the interval to 92 days and does not say the response is incomplete. Before writing to the vendor I ruled out a mistake on the bot's side: parameter names matched the specification, four alternative names gave the same answer, and a deliberately unknown parameter returned a different result.

I prepared fix requests for the vendor more than once, and some of them were met. One bank sent transactions without a payer, 0 of 265, so customer payments landed in the “Taxes” category; from my measurement the vendor fixed the field, the payer appeared in 283 of 283 transactions and the categories corrected themselves. The vendor opened webhook subscriptions for the bot's key once I showed the 403 refusal. The period truncation and an idempotency key for scheduled payments never came, and the bot covers those gaps itself: it splits the statement by month and treats the whole period as unavailable if even one slice fails.

One notification became rules the client owns

While the API work was going on, the client asked for a percentage of turnover. The gateway had no such field and no daily period, and the bot had no scheduler. Instead of one hard-coded message I proposed seven kinds of rules: incoming payment above an amount, balance below a threshold, a silent account, a digest, percentage of turnover, a payment reminder and a weekly summary. The client approved the whole set on 25 August and the rules went live the next day.

One wizard sets up every kind: which accounts, who gets it, the threshold, the day and the hour. A new kind is one more line in the list of steps, not a new screen. The wizard takes accounts and recipients from the bank and the access list each time it is shown, so it never offers a closed account or someone who has left.

The rules themselves are deterministic and know nothing about the network or the clock: a bank webhook, a timer and a button press all mean the same to them, “here is the data, look at it”. The repeat key is built from the financial fact, not from the run that found it, so one incoming payment never arrives twice.

On the night of 28 August the client set up six rules alone. The first live day gave four notifications and no delivery errors, and on 2 September the client rebuilt the income rule alone for a new set of accounts. By 29 September the bot had sent 1,344 notifications: 1,005 on incoming payments, 171 on low balances and 23 weekly digests.

AI proposes, code checks

The weekly summary in that set is written by a model, and it is not the only place where the bot uses AI. The client wanted AI: what they ordered was an AI agent that understands requests in plain language. Where it is really needed and where it is not, what goes into it and how its answer is checked, was mine to decide. As a result, AI sits where language is needed and never decides on its own. For a document request the model picks an intent from an allowed list of fields only, and the value comes from the company registry. A tax number the model pulls from free-form company details is accepted only if it appears verbatim in the text and passes the check digit; otherwise the ordinary parser runs. The model does not supply invoice figures either: the item is matched against a catalogue, and code reads the quantity and price itself.

For the summary the model receives ready figures and turns them into text; it is not asked to compute the turnover percentage, because an error in a text about money looks exactly like the truth to the reader. If the model does not answer, the notification still goes out, with the same figures and no summary.

The same rules apply to data that does not come from the model. Company details reach the database through a single checkpoint, wherever they come from. Tax and registration check digits and the link between an account and its bank code under the central bank's key block the write, while a check against the state register through DaData only warns: names and addresses cannot be normalised reliably, and a hard check would reject valid details. The details of the client's own companies are frozen in a snapshot, so a mismatch in a tax number, bank code or account is caught when the bot starts, before the first invoice rather than after the money has gone to the wrong place.

Personal data does not reach the model by design. The acceptance certificate with the seller's personal details is filled in by a wizard with no AI. From a payment description the summary takes only the contract reference: the first version sent descriptions in full, including cardholder names, and an allow list closed that before release. The bot records the cost of every call from the provider's response: 9 calls in production so far, $0.0030 in total.

Zero, empty and error: three different answers

Everything above rests on one distinction that the original scope did not have. It surfaced in review and manual checks: a failed request looked like an empty payment list, a zero balance or “Turnover: 0.00 RUB” while the bank was down. I added it along the way without breaking what had been accepted: data got a separate availability flag, and the check covered fourteen combinations of source availability and real emptiness instead of one happy path.

The bank itself put that boundary to the test. One account's transactions could not be read for 35 hours 5 minutes, 288 attempts in a row, while the other bank and the gateway's other methods kept answering, and the bot never told the client it had gone blind on that account. Five direct probes returned 502 in 0.13 to 0.17 seconds and the control bank returned 200 in 0.39 seconds, which ruled out a general network failure.

After that I closed the whole class in one branch. Account health became state, a cursor remembers the last complete point, the bot catches up over the failure window and sends one message on failure and one on recovery. On the very first weekend four outages of 45 to 75 minutes were announced and closed with a catch-up message. The client knew about the bank problems in those days: they were told separately, through management. Still, one incoming payment on 1 September fell outside the polling window before catch-up shipped, and the bot never reported it.

Writing to the bank: three outcomes and a test bench that never writes

Reading the bank is free, but a write into the client's contour cannot be undone. So a scheduled payment goes through a seven-step wizard, a summary and an explicit confirmation, and a write has three outcomes: applied, rejected or unknown. The vendor gave no idempotency key for these requests, so the bot never retries an unknown outcome automatically: the first request may have reached the bank even if the answer never came back.

Such paths cannot be tested against the live bank, so manual runs happen on a test bench: a separate copy of the bot behind a request interceptor, where reads go to the real service and the interceptor stops any write before it leaves. The rule came from a mistake: on 21 August the bench was started with the production key and without the interceptor, and a wizard run created a real scheduled payment for the client that then had to be cancelled. Later the same boundary caught a urllib behaviour: on a redirect the library turned POST into GET, and the bot would have reported success without writing anything. In fifteen minutes of manual running on the bench I found two defects that 737 tests and three review rounds had missed, so final acceptance still includes a manual run.

Recurring payments were settled with the same care. It was unclear whether the service creates the next record itself once a monthly payment is paid. Writing custom code on a guess risked a double write to the bank, and asking through management would take long. I made an assumption: touch nothing and watch what the API does. Three weeks later the answer was in the data: the paid monthly record stayed in August and the service created no new one. Then I built the solution on the bot's side: a paid recurring payment stays in the list with its next due date, and its reminder is switched off in the bot's own contour only, with no write to the bank. Since v0.8.9 on 21 September recurring payments work as intended.

A light version, a second client and where the system stands

Once the main bot had settled, the client asked for a light version: balances, incoming payments and the bank statement for two companies. On 8 September it went live in production next to the main bot, as a separate package in the same repository: it takes only the banking layer from the main bot, and the main bot did not change by a single line. The next day the client asked to switch off its notifications, since incoming payments arrived in both bots; the light version now waits for companies and keys from the vendor.

v0.8.11 shipped on 25 September: a second client got a full copy of the main bot with its own database and settings, and the main bot was not restarted. The second instance's banking section stays off until that company has its own key. Over six weeks the suite grew from 419 to 1,683 tests; the three known failures belong to the environment, not to the bot's code.

The gateway still never marks a response as incomplete, the vendor has not fixed the period truncation, and the client has still not approved the base for the turnover percentage, although the rule already computes that figure. The bot cannot prove the client's financial data is complete, so it says plainly what it does not know.

How it is built

Architecture
  • Every number has one owner (accounts and recipients come from the bank and the access list, company details from one registry), business rules stay apart from transport and text, participants plug in through interfaces, and rules are deterministic and testable without a network.
  • Ports and adapters: the core with rules, money and dates knows nothing of Telegram, the bank or the database; the bank, the model, storage and document rendering plug in as adapters.
  • Notification rules are pure functions of the account picture. A separate layer draws the text, so wording can change without touching logic.
  • One wizard for every kind of rule; a new kind is one more line in the list of steps.
  • The light version and the second client come from the same code: the light one takes only the banking layer, the second client gets its own database and settings.
Infrastructure
  • Python, aiogram 3, async SQLAlchemy, Alembic for migrations, SQLite with one database per company.
  • Each instance runs as its own systemd service on a VPS with its own token, database and settings; releasing one does not restart the others.
  • nginx with TLS receives bank webhooks, and every body is checked against an HMAC-SHA256 signature.
  • Wizard state lives in SQLite and survives a restart. Releases are tagged and go out in quiet windows.
  • The model is reached through OpenRouter; switching it takes one line of settings.
Fault tolerance
  • Unavailability is stored as its own flag, never as an empty answer; the check covers 14 combinations of availability and emptiness.
  • Every account has health, a cursor at the last complete point and catch-up over the failure window. The cursor moves only after delivery, so a Telegram failure cannot lose an incoming payment.
  • The statement is split by month and joined all or nothing: if one slice is missing, the whole period is unavailable.
  • A bank write goes through preview and confirmation, has three outcomes, and an unknown outcome is never retried on its own. The create button fires once per wizard run, even across a restart.
  • A model failure does not cancel a notification; in document search it switches to the ordinary parser with no AI.
Data and AI
  • The acceptance certificate with personal details is filled in by a wizard with no AI.
  • The weekly summary gets ready figures, and from a payment description only the contract reference.
  • The model picks an intent from an allowed list; a tax number from it is accepted only if it matches the text and the check digit; code supplies invoice figures.
  • Company details pass one checkpoint: check digits and the account to bank code link block the write, a state register check through DaData warns, and the client's own companies are checked against a snapshot at startup.
  • Before choosing the model, three candidates ran the same 35 requests, typos and slang included.
  • The cost of every call is recorded from the provider's response: 9 calls in production, $0.0030 in total.
Decisions and assumptions
  • Do not cover the vendor's category with a custom rule: after a vendor fix it would corrupt correct data.
  • Split the statement by month, not by quarter: three times the cost, but it cannot break silently if the vendor lowers the limit.
  • Recurring payments: first watch what the API does, and only then build the solution on the bot's side.
  • Switch reminders off in the bot's own contour, not with a bank write: writes cannot be undone, and the vendor's API does not always follow its specification.
  • Take upstairs only what cannot be settled without the client, such as the base for the turnover percentage; close the rest with a written assumption.
