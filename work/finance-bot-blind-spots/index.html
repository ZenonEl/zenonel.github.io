When no bank data looked like no money

I led the financial part of a production Telegram back office built on a thinly documented gateway to Sber and Alfa-Bank. The difficult part was keeping a missing response from becoming a false financial fact, while writes still landed in the client's live bank contour.

The bot grew from documents into live money

The project began as an assistant for invoices and contracts, with separate checks around bank details. It later grew into a production back office for balances, statements, planned payments, notifications and access control. The client entered the production bot on 18 August 2026 and used the financial functions in daily work.

The bank layer reads Sber and Alfa-Bank through a third-party gateway with thin documentation. An empty list can mean that there are no operations, that one account is unavailable, or that the gateway silently cut the period. A write is riskier: the service has no delete operation, so a payment created in the client's contour cannot be undone through the same API.

Management handled the client relationship. I chose how we would distinguish missing data from empty data, required a preview before writes, tested the critical paths by hand and decided when a change was ready for production. AI sessions implemented the code. A separate reviewer checked it with shell access. A colleague's webhook and access-control code is part of the system; our work there was review and correction, not authorship.

A bank failure must not become a zero

The first versions flattened too many states. A failed request could appear as an empty payment list, a zero balance or a screen saying there was nobody to ask. The closing check eventually covered fourteen combinations of source availability and real emptiness instead of testing only the happy path.

The sharpest incident lasted 35 hours and 5 minutes across 288 attempts: operations from one Sber account failed while Alfa-Bank and the other gateway methods answered normally. The bot never told the client that the account was blind. Five direct probes returned 502 in 0.13 to 0.17 seconds while the control bank returned 200 in 0.39 seconds, which ruled out a general network failure.

I chose to close the class in one branch: one read per run, account health stored as state, a cursor for the last complete point, backfill over the outage window, and one message when a source fails and another when it catches up. On the first weekend after deployment, four outages lasting 45 to 75 minutes were all announced and closed with a recovery message.

The earlier blind period still had a cost: the bot could not distinguish an empty account from an unavailable one. That ambiguity is why the later design treats availability as state rather than inferring it from an empty response.

We had to prove where the defect lived

A wide statement request returned 25 operations where month-by-month requests returned 275. Thirty-four manual measurements established the hidden rule: the gateway clamped the interval to 92 days from its start without saying that the response was incomplete.

I asked for the alternative explanations to be tested before blaming the vendor. The parameter names matched the specification, the end date worked inside the 92-day window, four alternative parameter names returned the same result, and an intentionally unknown start parameter produced a different response. The workaround slices statements by month and fails the whole period if any slice is unavailable. Quarterly slices would have been three times cheaper, but a two-day reduction in the vendor limit would break them silently.

Another defect put incoming payments into the wrong category because Sber operations arrived without a payer. The full sample showed the payer in 0 of 265 operations at one bank and in all 18 sampled at the other. We did not add our own classifier on top of the vendor's categories. After the vendor fixed its side, the payer appeared in 283 of 283 Sber operations, where it had been absent in 301 of 301 before the fix, and the categories corrected with it.

This boundary is still imperfect. As of 9 September, another wide query could drop all 210 operations from the last month. From outside the system we cannot distinguish whether the truncation happens in the gateway or inside the bank.

One requested alert became rules the client controls

A request for a percentage-of-turnover alert exposed a larger need. The gateway had no such field, no daily period and the bot had no scheduler. I proposed seven configurable rule types instead of hard-coding one notification, and the client agreed to the set.

A webhook and a scheduled poll both mean the same thing to the rule engine: wake up, read the current picture across accounts and evaluate pure rules. Deduplication is based on the financial fact rather than the run that found it. On the first night after release, the client created six rules without us. Their first live day produced four triggers, twenty delivery keys and no send errors; later the client rebuilt an income rule after the account set changed.

The weekly narrative is the only path where client data reaches a model. The first version sent complete payment purposes, including cardholder names. I replaced that with a whitelist parser that extracts only a contract reference. The summary uses Gemini Flash Lite through OpenRouter, and its first recorded live run cost $0.00035 according to the provider response.

One business rule remains unresolved: the percentage base was never agreed with the client even though the rule can already compute a figure. The site does not present that calculation as accepted policy.

A bank write has an unknown outcome too

Every planned-payment write goes through a preview and an explicit confirmation. The result is one of three states: applied, refused or unknown. An unknown result is never retried automatically because the first request may have reached the bank even when the response did not reach us.

The development stand forwards reads to the live service but blocks every POST before it leaves the machine. That boundary caught a redirect behaviour where Python's urllib changed a POST into a GET: the bot could have reported success although nothing had been written.

Manual checking still mattered after automation. Fifteen minutes on the stand found two defects that 737 tests and three review rounds had missed. Final acceptance still includes a manual run.

Where the system stands

By 21 September 2026 the production bot was on v0.8.10. The test suite had grown from 419 to 1,680 tests in five weeks, with three known external failures in the final run. The client managed access through the bot and maintained notification rules without asking us to edit configuration.

The system still depends on a gateway whose incomplete answers are not self-describing. Wide-period truncation remains open, the first blind interval cannot be reconstructed completely, and the percentage base still needs a client decision. The external API has not become reliable, and the bot cannot prove that the client's financial records are complete.
