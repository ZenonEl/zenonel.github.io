W-04Case

The product metric counted a refusal as a successful answer

This was my first project after coming back to the team, and the first where I learned to work with AI agents. In a SaaS that shows how AI assistants see a website, the main metric looked for the brand name in the model's answer and counted a refusal as a mention: 14 of 15 where there was not a single real one. I moved it to a verification service with a model judge and two rubrics, calibrated it on a 44-case set, and carried the same principle, no grounds means refusal, into article generation.

My first project on the team

This product was my first project after I came back to the team in March 2026. It was also where I started working with Claude Code in a terminal: I had almost no experience with agent-driven development, and the team did not use Codex yet. Dailies for the project only appeared on 6 May, a month after the start.

Another project came before it, a content production pipeline for SEO agencies. From 20 March to 10 April it gathered 398 commits, and then it got stuck. Management looked into it and found serious problems in the architecture, although I had managed to keep some parts in good shape. On management's decision a new project started on 8 April, built on the first one's groundwork; that is the one this case describes.

The main lesson of that period is about data: every value has one source, and the interface shows what that source returned, not a guess of its own. The metric story below is about exactly such a guess.

The product and who worked on it

The product checks how ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Copilot see a website: a checkup runs as a graph of 19 steps, and the content side writes articles that are not published without human approval. One of the report's main figures showed whether the models mention the client's brand.

It was a team project. Management set the direction and approved production releases, and a colleague ran the content plan service. The backend and the AI side were mine, along with the final say on merging changes. Work ran in several agent chats, separately for frontend, backend and CI; AI agents wrote the code, and every change went through subagent review until LGTM. Of the project's 858 commits, 626 are mine.

14 of 15 where there were none

On 14 May I ran the product against my own personal site: the models certainly do not know its brand, so the honest answer would be “I don't know”. A subagent acting as an outside consultant read all 15 answers. It found no real mention at all, yet the tool reported 14 of 15, that is 93%.

Inside, the picture was worse than the figure: in 11 answers the model refused outright, in three it confused or invented things, and one was a correct denial. Five refusals were even scored as positive sentiment. The root was a single line: a mention counted if the brand name appeared in the answer text, and “I don't know brand X” contains it. The issue went to P0.

A judge instead of a substring

Management set the direction: do not patch the heuristic with yet another list of denial phrases, build verification services for the whole class of tasks that need a judge rather than a text search. On 15 May the service went live. Every claim passes through a verification gateway, every call is logged, and a run has a budget of $0.10. The judge returns one of five outcomes: a real mention, a bare name, a pivot to another brand, a refusal or an invention; only the first counts.

The same day the product path moved to the service, and the substring search and the denial list were deleted. Two decisions here were mine. First, the service's state (did it run?) is kept apart from the verdict (what did the judge decide?), so a failure never looks like “not mentioned”. Second, if the product is less than 0.6 sure about the brand itself, the probes do not run and the report says plainly that the brand was not recognised.

Strictness broke the well-known brands

On 18 May the judge got a strict rubric and brand facts from the shared board of verified facts; by default an answer counted as an invention. Across five sites with 15 probes each, false mentions on an unknown brand fell from 80% to zero. But a well-known brand then got only 8 of 15, so false rejections appeared.

On 19 May there were two rubrics: one for a known brand and a strict one for an unknown brand, while the service's outside contract stayed the same. I wrote the acceptance criteria in advance: recall on known brands at least 80%, false mentions on unknown ones at most 10%. The second reviewer found that the mode could switch to “known” on a single hint about the niche; now without at least one verified fact from the board the brand counts as unknown.

I checked this on a synthetic set: 40 hand-written cases, 25 known and 15 unknown. The strict prompt gave 64.3% recall, the two rubrics gave 100%, and there were no false positives on unknown brands in either. The final version on an extended set of 44 cases: recall 16 of 16, one false mention of 17, about a cent per run. I accepted the three remaining classification errors on purpose: their outcome is the same, the brand is not mentioned.

On 25 May one more loose end closed: if the probes did not run, the brand authority score cannot rise above 60. On a live run it fell from 100 to 60, which was the right figure.

No grounds means refusal

On 18 June management set a rule for article generation: do not invent. Where to hold it was my decision. The gate sits in the run itself, before the graph is built: a brief without the required structure is rejected, the model is not called, no internal currency is charged, and the run keeps a reason code.

On 29 and 30 June the other side of the same problem appeared: Russian articles broke off mid-word and still went on to review. Every step hit its own token limit, 4,000 for the draft and 6,000 for polishing. I derived the budget from the article length: the tokens needed equal the word count times 1.5 times a language factor (2.0 for Cyrillic), and the budget is twice what is needed, within 4,000 to 16,000. The limit here caps cost rather than setting length: only actual use is paid for. Ten unit tests hold the formula.

If the model still hits the limit, the run no longer pretends the article is finished: it is rejected with a “text truncated” code.

Splitting the orchestrator: a live run found what the tests missed

In late May, after management's directive of one engine for two markets, the checkup job itself came up. The code map showed one 976-line file with seven responsibilities, and six imports led from the domain layer straight into the database. On 26 and 27 May I split it in five phases: six use cases in the application layer with 24 tests, ports for sites and progress, and a save node that issues a command instead of writing to the database. At the merge the file weighed 661 lines and tests went to 3,104. The second reviewer found that when saving failed, a run stayed in the “running” status forever, and that was closed before the merge.

On 28 May a live run showed that the split had silently broken saving results: the LangGraph graph dropped a key not declared in the state description, and the tests called the node directly and did not see it. The fix went out the same day, and a rule stayed: a graph is tested through a real graph, not by calling a single function.

The same change added a test that reads the domain files and fails if any of them imports the database session at module level. The rule is deliberately narrow: ORM models and some utilities still lived in infrastructure then, and other imports remained. The test ran locally and before every push; the project's GitHub CI pipelines never got going. Following the same pattern, the article brief and localisation were extracted by mid-June, and by 11 June the services directory held 15 contexts, some written almost entirely by the colleague.

Where it stands

The product runs as a production stand without a public launch. The 29 September snapshot shows 5 tenants, 8 checkups averaging 154 seconds and $0.074, and 15 article generations: one published, 11 waiting for human approval, two rejected and one failed. Cost is visible for each of the 23 steps that call a model.

The result has limits. The calibration set is synthetic and small. The original 14 of 15 run was not repeated on the same site after the fix: the brand recognition gate simply did not launch the probes. The effect on users' decisions was not measured.

The project went better than the first version, but at some point it was simply dropped and never reached a public launch. For me it remained the main project of that period: it is where I learned to work with agents.

How it is built

Architecture
  • Verification, crawling and search live in separate contexts behind stable gateways with a shared evidence contract.
  • A shared board of verified facts: some steps write to it, others read it, and the judge relies on it.
  • The checkup and article generation are LangGraph graphs of pluggable steps: 19 in the checkup, 11 in content, the last one with human approval.
  • A modular monolith on a service template: domain, application, infrastructure, API; contexts talk only through a port and a single adapter.
Infrastructure
  • FastAPI, async SQLAlchemy, Alembic, LangGraph with state persisted in PostgreSQL, Celery and Redis, three SvelteKit interfaces.
  • Docker Compose: database, Redis, migrations, backend and worker as separate services.
  • Releases through semantic-release and a separate production deployment workflow.
Fault tolerance
  • Loud failures with a reason code instead of a quiet result: brief rejected, text truncated, brand not recognised.
  • A budget on every verification run.
  • Once a Celery worker started without a queue flag hung silently and 764 messages piled up; since then this is a regression class of its own.
  • 424 test files and snapshot tests on stable contracts.
  • A regression test on a real graph rather than a node call, and protection against an endless “running” status when saving fails.
Data and AI
  • The judge runs on gpt-4o-mini, articles are written by Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5, all models through OpenRouter.
  • Cost is recorded for each of the 23 steps that call a model; a checkup costs $0.074 on average.
  • No article is published without human approval.
Decisions and assumptions
  • Keep the service's state apart from the judge's verdict.
  • A 0.6 confidence threshold for recognising the brand: a general label beats a wrong figure.
  • Without a verified fact the brand counts as unknown.
  • Accept the judge's three remaining errors: their outcome is the same.
  • Treat the token limit as a cost cap, not a target length.
  • Production releases and the content plan language were management's call.
  • The boundary rule is narrow: only the database session is forbidden while models and utilities live in infrastructure.
